## 1 Introduction 

## 1.1 Public Key Infrastructures 

A Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) enables the establishment and management of trust relationships between different entities. 

A PKI is based on the principle of asymmetric cryptography. With asymmetric cryptography, a key pair is created. This key pair consists of a private (i.e. secret) key and a public key. Data encrypted with the public key can only be decrypted with the corresponding private key. Encryption is used to keep data confidential. In addition, it is possible to digitally sign data with the same private key. The associated public key can then be used to check whether the data has been changed since the signature. Digital signatures are used to make modifications of data transparent and thus, to ensure the integrity of the signed data. 

The most important tool within a PKI is the certificate. A certificate binds the public key of a key pair to the identity of an entity (e.g. person or organization). I.e., a certificate contains the public key of a key pair together with other information, such as who issued the certificate, for whom it was issued and how long the certificate is valid. A certificate is issued by a Certification Authority (CA) and signed by this Certification Authority with its private key. It is the certification of trust in the certificate holder by the issuing Certification Authority. 

Although there are many different structures of PKI, this document only considers hierarchical PKIs. In a hierarchical organized PKI the topmost instance of the PKI is the so-called root-CA. The root-CA serves as anchor of trust within a PKI and signs its own certificate. Furthermore, it either signs and disseminates end entity certificates or, and this is the general case, signs and disseminates one or more certificates for so-called sub-CAs. Sub-CAs, whose associated certificates were signed with the private key of the root-CA, can in-turn use their private key to sign certificates of other entities. Theoretically, a chain like this can be any length as long as it starts with the root certificate. To check the authenticity and trustworthiness of a certificate from a PKI, the certificates of all CAs that lie between the entity to be checked and the root-CA must be verified. 

The business of a PKI is a business of trust. This document contributes to the establishment and management of trust in a PKI in two ways: 

First, the document at hand defines requirements for secure operation of a CA. By implementing these organizational and technical security measures, security level ‘high’ according to Part 1 of Technical Guideline (BSI-TR-03107) is achieved by a CA. 

Secondly, this document enables the transparent and comparable demonstration that a CA effectively and completely implements these security measures and thus, achieves security level ‘high’. In order to do so, an audit of the CA is performed based on the requirements from this document by a trusted independent auditor and certified by a trusted certification body. 

Both together build the basis which legitimates the trust in a CA. 

## 1.2 Scope of this document 

This document is part 1 of the Technical Guideline series (BSI-TR-03145) defining requirements for the operation of a CA with security level 'high'. The other documents of the series consider additional or modified requirements for specific business cases. Unless stated otherwise in specific parts of this series, security level ‘high’ applies for all documents in this series. 

The document at hand describes generic requirements for the secure operation of a CA. Therefore, a CA shall implement an Information Security Management System (ISMS) in order to establish, maintain and improve information security.

## 1.3 Audit and certification 

Audit and certification according to this Technical Guideline create trust in a CA by providing evidence that a CA is compliant with the requirements from this Technical Guideline and achieves security level ‘high’. 

As mentioned above, it is possible for CAs to be certified by an accredited ISO/IEC 27001 certification body using this document, in conjunction with its references to (ISO27099), as sector-specific requirements. This implies that the scope of the ISMS of the CA includes the entire CA operation and includes the following requirements: 

- the CA is audited by an independent, external and accredited ISO/IEC 27001 auditor, 

- that this audit is performed and repeated on a regular basis (annual surveillance audit), 

- that the scope of this audit also covers the requirements defined in the document at hand.

## **1.3.1 Scope of audit and certification** 

This Document, Part 1 of BSI TR-03145, defines requirements for the secure operation of a single CA in a PKI. This CA SHALL be uniquely identified in its own certificate as subject. Furthermore, its relationship within the PKI SHALL be clearly specified. This SHALL consist of a complete description of 

- all CAs the PKI consists of whereas these CAs are all uniquely identified in their certificate as subject, 

- the PKI hierarchy, and 

- all other PKI components or services that may be relevant for the security of the CA, such as for example Registration Authority, Directory Service or CRL Signer (if supported). 

As the business of root-CA and sub-CA is very similar, almost all requirements specified in the document at hand apply to both. Therefore, the general term CA is used in this document. For the few aspects which might differ, it will be mentioned explicitly. 

Despite the general character of the requirements from this document, the individual implementation of these requirements vary. In general, the individual implementations of technical or organizational security measures differ between root-CA and sub-CA and sometimes even between different sub-CAs within the same PKI. I.e. this document is meant as the basis for the audit and certification of a single CA. Nonetheless, an audit according to Part 1 or other application specific parts MAY be performed for more than one single

## 1.4 Concept and structure of this document 

In order to ease the application of this document in conjunction with (ISO27099), this document mainly follows the structure of (ISO27099). 

However, like previous versions of this Technical Guideline, this version of the Technical Guideline continues to use the term “requirement” instead of the term “control procedure” used in (ISO27099). 

The control procedures from Section 7 of (ISO27099) constitute the basis of the requirements for secure CA operation defined by this Technical Guideline. In detail, Sections 2, 3 and 4 of this document adopt and amend the control procedures of Sections 7.2 to 7.15 of (ISO27099) to define the requirements that SHALL be fulfilled for compliance of a CA with this Technical Guideline. In order to be compliant with this Technical Guideline a CA SHALL fulfil all requirements from this document. This SHALL include those requirements which are implicitly contained in this document as a reference to (ISO27099) as well as those requirements which are explicitly stated in this document. 

Throughout this document, “(if supported)” is to be understood as conditional requirement(s). For example, the requirements concerning certificate renewal, see section 4.2, are conditional. That means, a CA MAY allow certificate renewal. If a CA allows certificate renewal, then all requirements specified in section 4.2 SHALL be fulfilled by that CA. Otherwise, these requirement(s) are not applicable. 

To enable the balance between flexibility and precision so-called “assignments”[1] are used within this document. These are to be filled in by the CA, thereby enforcing a specification of a single procedure, but also allowing different solutions within defined limits. 

Note that the standard (ISO27099) defines a Policy Authority (PA) as a dedicated role. This Technical Guideline does not require the existence of a dedicated Policy Authority. For control procedures from (ISO27099) that require the role of a Policy Authority and that SHALL be fulfilled as requirement according to this document, the responsibilities ascribed to a Policy Authority SHALL be fulfilled by the CA or another appropriate instance under responsibility of the CA.

## 2 General requirements for Certification Authorities 

A CA shall be considered trustworthy by subscribers and relying parties for any of its services. To ensure trustworthiness various aspects have to be considered. This includes for example operational security, security of the infrastructure, physical security and trustworthy personnel. Each of the following subsections covers such security requirements. 

## 2.1 Cryptographic measures 

The use of inappropriate cryptographic algorithms or parameters facilitates the compromise of certificates, signatures, hashes or encryption. Therefore, the CA shall ensure that appropriate cryptographic algorithms and parameters are used. The algorithms and parameters shall comply with the current state of the art and shall be based on well-known Technical Guidelines and standards such as for example (BSI-TR-03116), (BSITR-02102). 

Concerning the cryptographic measures, the following requirement applies: 

- **2.1A1** The CA SHALL ensure that the cryptographic algorithms and their parameters comply with the newest editions of the following documents: [assignment: technical guidelines or standards for cryptographic algorithms and recommended key lengths], which SHALL be chosen depending on the business case of the CA. 

The CA SHALL check the cryptographic algorithms and their parameters referring to the newest editions: 

- Each time a cryptographic algorithm is newly implemented. 

- When an incident of a used algorithm becomes public. 

- Regularly, in a period of [assignment: number of days, weeks or months]. 

## 2.2 Certification Practice Statement and Certificate Policy management 

According to (RFC3647) a Certificate Policy (CP) is a _'named set of rules that indicates applicability of a certificate to a particular community and/or class application with common security requirements'_ . A Certificate Policy states "what is to be adhered to". 

According to (ISO27099) a Certification Practice Statement (CPS) is issued by a CA and _‘defines the equipment, policies, and procedures the CA uses to satisfy the requirements specified in the certificate policies’_ . In this context, a CPS is more refined than the CP it conforms to, and describes individual measures in the key and certificate life cycle and their technical realization. A Certification Practice Statement states "how it is adhered to". 

This Section refers to Section 7.2 of (ISO27099) and addresses requirements concerning both, Certificate Policy and Certification Practice Statement. With respect to Certificate Policy and Certification Practice Statement, the following requirements apply: 

- **2.2A1** CP and CPS SHALL be part of the documentation which is maintained within the ISMS. 

- **2.2A2** Within a PKI the CPs and CPSs SHALL be compliant. Before issuing a certificate to a CA, the issuing CA SHALL ensure that CP and CPS of the subscribing CA are consistent with its own CP and CPS. A subscribing CA SHALL ensure that its own CP and CPS are consistent with CP and CPS of the issuing CA.

## 3 Key life cycle management 

A cryptographic key has several states during its life. The typical key lifecycle in the context of CA operation comprises the following states: key generation, key storage, key backup, key recovery, key distribution, key usage, key archival and key destruction. 

The CA shall ensure that the security of cryptographic keys is maintained during the whole key lifecycle. This holds for the CA’s own key as well as for those keys that are owned by subjects and the CA issues corresponding certificates for. 

In this section requirements concerning the key lifecycle management are specified. Thereby, requirements concerning the management of the CA’s own key relate to Section 7.13 of (ISO27099) and are addressed in subsection 3.1. Requirements concerning subject keys relate to Section 7.14 of (ISO27099) and are specified in subsection 3.2

## 4 Certificate life cycle management 

Like cryptographic keys, certificates have several states during their life. According to (ISO27099) a certificate may be pre-valid, live, suspended (if supported), revoked or expired. During each of these states the security and trustworthiness of certificates shall be maintained be the CA.

## 4.1 Subject registration

A subscriber shall provide documentation for identification to the CA, while the CA checks the identity of this subscriber based on the provided documentation. - **4.9A3** The Servers hosting the maintained revocation information SHALL be hardened against failure and attacks. The hardening against failure and attacks SHALL be based on the risk analysis. 

## 4.10 Validation Model 

The validation model determines the conditions under which a signature is considered valid. Usually the Shell or Hybrid Model are used. 

Within the Shell Model a signature is valid if all certificates in the certificate chain are valid at the time of validation. 

Hybrid Model and Modified Shell Model are often used synonymously. The Hybrid Model denotes that a signature is valid if all certificates in the certificate chain are valid at the time of signing. 

A certificate is valid if the time of signing or validation is within the validity period of the certificate, the certificate is not revoked (cf. revocationDate according to (RFC5280)) at time of signing or validation and it is not invalid (cf. invalidityDate according to (RFC5280)) at time of signing or validation. Note that a certificate can be declared invalid in retrospective. 

Concerning the validation model the following requirements apply: 

- **4.10A1** Within the whole PKI in which the CA is active, one validation model SHALL be consistently applied. That means that the CA SHALL ensure a consistent application of the validation model for all of its operations such that the validity periods of all certificates in a certificate chain are chosen according to the chosen validation model. 

- **4.10A2** In general, the Shell Model SHOULD be used. If the CA uses a different validation model, the CA SHALL justify its reasons.
