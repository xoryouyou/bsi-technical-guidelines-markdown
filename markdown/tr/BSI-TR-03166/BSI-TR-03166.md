Introduction 

## 1 Introduction 

## 1.1 Motivation 

In the last decade, the development of computing devices has been focused on accessibility, usability and versatility; modern smartphones are the best example for this trend. Due to the availability of new technologies and a continuously growing variety of online services, our behaviour and daily life have changed significantly. In the context of digitalisation, more and more services are offered over the internet. The increasing availability of internet access and the functionalities of current devices, especially mobile devices like smartphones, have transformed them into control centres for many applications. These manifold applications expose users to higher risks. Indeed, successful authentication on a single device is sufficient to access financial services, health applications, public authority services (eGovernment), communication and smart home functionalities. 

Classic protection methods against unauthorized access are strong passwords or special hardware tokens, epitomizing the authentication factors knowledge and possession. As an alternative, biometric technologies like face or fingerprint recognition are steadily gaining attention as a more convenient and hence widely accepted authentication factor. Nowadays almost all devices like tablets, smartphones and laptops have some form of integrated biometric subsystem for enrolment and authentication on the device. Considering the increasing damage potential of current online services, it is vital to accurately assess the security level of the authentication technologies and processes, which are in use to protect the online services. 

In contrast to the clear advantage of biometric technologies in terms of convenience and speed as compared to classic authentication methods, assessing their real-life security level is a highly challenging and still heavily debatable topic. Many current security standards and normative regulations consider only the robustness against a brute-force attack when evaluating the security level of an authentication method. Even for classic authentication methods like passwords, the focus on entropy neglects many practically relevant attack vectors, like easily guessable passwords, various snooping techniques, etc. For biometric methods, this simplified approach would definitely fall too short. Biometric performance always depends on several parameters like sensor properties, biometric comparison algorithms, environmental factors or the biometric features of the data subject. In addition, the overall security level of biometric methods in unsupervised settings is highly dependent on their resistance against various attacks based on artificial or augmented biometric characteristics. 

## 1.2 Scope 

The scope of this technical guideline is to define requirements for biometric authentication / verification systems including their subsystems in terms of the: 

1. biometric performance 

2. attack resistance against Presentation Attacks (PA) 

Both parts, biometric performance and attack resistance, are critical for the security assessment of a biometric system and have to be evaluated properly. Due to several subsystems within the biometric authentication / verification process, this document focusses on Presentation Attacks at the Sensor level, as shown in Figure 1 for the modalities finger and face. Currently, all other possible attack vectors, like data handling and storage issues, are out of scope or can be covered by common IT security measures and methods.

Biometrics as an authentication factor is from its characteristics different compared to knowledge- and possession-based authentication factors. Due to its probabilistic character biometrics has to deal with uncertainties, like natural variations of a person or changes associated with the presentation process. Therefore, biometrics has to be handled differently compared to other authentication factors, e.g. introducing thresholds for the biometric comparison. 

This technical guideline is mainly directed towards manufacturers and developers of devices and services and service provider implementing biometrics as an authentication / verification with the aim to achieve a certification for their biometric authentication / verification system (1:1 comparison). This technical guideline often uses the smartphone as a daily life example, but it does not mean that the technical guideline is only applicable to smartphones. 

The main evaluation framework is based on the definition of BSI Biometric Assurance Levels (BALs), which are partially derived from eIDAS Regulations 910/2014 [1] and 2024/1183 [2] and BSI TR-03107 [3] and their requirements on authentication factors and interpreted for the peculiarities of biometrics. With this principle our technical guideline can be applied to more use cases and applications than defined by eIDAS and BSI TR-03107. 

This technical guideline does not apply to identification processes (1:N comparison) and biometric authentication scenarios regulated by BSI TR-03121 [4] and the corresponding conformity specifications defined in BSI TR-03122 [5]. Such scenarios are for example border control, German identity Documents, alien register enrolment and immigration authorities. 

Note: This technical long as it is based on a 1:1 comparison as stated before.

## 1.3 Framework 

This technical guideline consists of two parts: 

- Technical Guideline BSI TR-03166 

- Evaluation Guidance (EG) 

The first document defines the requirements of the biometric authentication / verification system. The second document is the EG, which defines the metrics and procedures regarding the evaluation of the biometric performance and the Presentation Attack Detection (PAD). 

## 1.4 System Model 

The most prominent examples for biometric authentication processes in daily life are mobile devices and in particular smartphones. These complex systems are able to run a huge variety of services and applications, which leads to many potential threats, if the security of such a device is compromised. 

A correspondingly secure authentication mechanism in such systems is equipped with systems for both biometric decision including a Presentation Attack Detection (PAD) decision, with its subsystems (see Figure 1, for details subsection 2.2). 

A bona fide user (see [6], [7]) can typically use authentication factors to gain access to, e.g.: 

- specific locations (rooms, buildings) 

- devices (smartphones, personal computers) 

- services (login processes) 

As a first step, a specific authentication factor has to be registered. For the registration of a biometric authentication factor, this step is called enrolment. Afterwards, this authentication factor can be used for authentication and verification purposes. 

Figure 2 depicts a two-step authentication, which can be found, e.g., on smartphones. In the first step (access-level), the data subject unlocks the device by using an authentication factor, which can be a password / PIN (Personal Identification Number), a hardware token (possession-based factor) and biometrics. 

Biometrics can be used to improve the security, if used as an additional authentication factor in a two-factor authentication (2FA) or multi-factor authentication process. Access to the device can be granted (Figure 2 Layer 1) as a result of these operations, and further services are accessible, depending on additional security measures (Figure 2 Layer 2 service-level). Additional security measures in the second layer can be authentication factors independent from the authentication factor of the first layer.

## 1.5 Overview 

## 1.5.1 How to use this Technical Guideline 

This technical guideline serves as a developers developing devices with authentication / verification processes using biometrics. 

This document considers a generic system, where an authentication process is realised by using biometrics. This process can be built up of different authentication layers (access-level and service-level) as shown in Figure 2. In general, the authentication process of each layer can be executed using a possession-, knowledge- and / or biometrics-based factor. 

A successful first layer authentication enables a user to unlock the device. Depending on the security requirements of different applications and services on the device a second authentication layer can be necessary, which can use multiple authentication factors. The strength of the authentication combined methods has to satisfy the overall security requirements. Hardware and software developers have to implement the hardware-, software-components and additional security measures in authentication processes necessary to fulfil the requirements defined for the targeted Biometric Assurance Level (BAL 1, 2 or 3). 

Beginning with chapter 2 all requirements containing normative wording are starting with a bold identifier. Requirements which have to be considered within the certification process are repeated in normative parts of this Technical Guideline. The used abbreviations are not explained in further detail, but it is possible to divide the requirements in general requirements independent from BALs and requirements specific to reach a certain BAL as specified in this document. 

For clarification: a device using a biometric verification as an authentication factor does not need to support all BALs. In consequence, only services and applications up to the supported BAL can be used with the device using biometrics. 

The biometric functionality, performance and PAD functionality will be evaluated by an approved testing laboratory. Requirements for test laboratories and evaluators are defined by additional BSI documents[1] . The testing specification is published in a separate document. 

## 1.5.2 Target group 

This technical guideline allows biometric authentication service- / system-providers and developers to check the requirements defined for a particular BAL in terms of biometric performance and PAD functionality. With this knowledge, developers and manufacturers can set up their biometric authentication system to be compliant to the desired BAL. 

## 1.5.3 Target of Evaluation (TOE) 

The elements of the TOE have to be defined in the evaluation documentation, including their configuration. 

In this Technical Guideline a biometric authentication system and thus the target of evaluation (TOE) consists of all components that are directly required for the biometric authentication process and presentation attack detection (see Figure 1). 

For biometric systems operating with various sample acquisition subsystems, e.g., cloud based biometric systems that use a wide range of smartphones or webcams, it is allowed to keep the sample acquisition

## 2 Biometric Verification as an Authentication Factor (Informative) 

To achieve conformance with different standards and normative texts, this technical guideline needs to consider single- and multi-factor authentication processes. Possible authentication factors are based on possession, knowledge or biometrics. A detailed description for possession- and knowledge-based authentication factors can be found in BSI TR-03107-1 [3]. 

This chapter includes descriptions, definitions, requirements and recommendations. The intention of the following chapters is to introduce different concepts and possibilities to implement biometrics in an authentication / verification process. Further, a generic biometric system with its subsystems is briefly described. The overall intention of this chapter is to introduce a general biometric system with its different subsystems, possible attack vectors to attack such a system and established international standards regarding metrics for performance evaluation and comparison metrics for Presentation Attack Detection (PAD) mechanisms, which are not typically used in a security evaluation outside of biometric systems. 

With respect to the certification process the following subchapters are INFORMATIVE , even if normative key words are used. In future versions of this technical guideline this chapter can be changed to a normative chapter. 

## 2.1 Authentication Factors 

Authentication factors can be grouped into three categories: knowledge, possession and biometrics. In other words, these three groups can be described as something you know, something you possess and something you are. The categories possession and knowledge are more precisely defined and described with their corresponding requirements in BSI TR-03107-1 [3]. Biometrics as an authentication factor is addressed in this technical guideline in the following sections. 

A general ranking of the different authentication factors across all authentication categories is not feasible, because every factor has its particular requirements regarding environment, usability, costs and the necessary level of security. The major advantage of biometrics, the convenience that natural features of a person cannot be lost or forgotten, is also its biggest disadvantage. 

Due to the limited number of biometric characteristics, it is not possible to change biometric features arbitrarily often if a sample is stolen. A possibility to hamper attacks with stolen biometric references / templates can be the use of template protection mechanisms. Suitable methods are still under development and investigation. 

Therefore, secure storage solutions are of particular importance, but not part of this technical guideline. 

In addition, the probabilistic character of biometric methods gives rise to the possibility that persons with similar biometric characteristics (for some modalities observable on twins) are recognized as the genuine data subject. 

When aiming for a certain assurance level, single factor, multi-factor or multi-stage authentication processes can combine the authentication factors from the three categories in accordance with specific technical guidelines and / or other related standards. 

## 2.1.1 Biometric Verification for Authentication 

Biometrics can be used as an authentication factor in a single- and a multi-factor authentication process.

Multiple biometric characteristics of the same modality are used for the verification. An addition to the multi-instance method can be the multi-presentation scheme, where the same biometric characteristic is presented multiple times to the capture device. 

If possible, a biometric sample can be captured with multiple different sensors at the same time.

## 2.1.1.5 Error Rates in Biometrics 

In this section, a general introduction to error rates used in biometrics is given and is INFORMATIVE . The terms and definitions are in agreement with the metrics for presentation attack detection in ISO / IEC 30107-3:2023 [7] and the description of metrics for the evaluation of biometric performance in ISO/IEC 19795-1:2021 [48]. 

1. The false match rate (FMR) is the proportion of the completed biometric non-mated comparison trials that result in a false match. 

2. The false non-match rate (FNMR) is the proportion of the completed biometric mated comparison trials that result in a false non-match. 

Often the terms FMR / FNMR and FAR / FRR are used interchangeably, but there is a significant difference between them. On the one hand, FMR and FNMR can be related to the matching algorithm (algorithm level error). On the other hand, the FAR and FRR also take into account those attempts for which the system fails to generate a signal or fails to extract features. Therefore, the FAR and FRR describe the system as a whole

The biometric verification subsystem includes hardware and software. Therefore, the FAR and FRR have to be applied. Beside the overall error rates, specific error rates for PAD can be defined, but are not relevant for the vulnerability analysis in this technical guideline: 

1. The attack presentation classification error rate (APCER) is defined by the proportion of attack presentations using the same PAI species which are incorrectly classified as genuine presentations in a specific scenario. 

2. The bona fide presentation classification error rate (BPCER) is the proportion of bona fide presentations incorrectly classified as presentation attacks in a specific scenario. In a bona fide attempt, a valid identity claiming is performed with the genuine biometric characteristics belonging to this identity. 

3. The attack presentation non-response rate (APNRR) is the proportion of attack presentations using the same PAI species that cause no response at the PAD subsystem or data capture subsystem. 

4. The bona fide presentation non-response rate (BPNRR) is the proportion of bona fide presentations that cause no response at the PAD subsystem or data capture subsystem. 

5. The attack presentation acquisition rate (APAR) is the proportion of attack presentations using the same PAI species from which the data capture subsystem acquires a biometric sample of sufficient quality. 

6. The impostor attack presentation match rate (IAPMR) is the proportion of impostor attack presentations using the same PAI species in which the target reference is matched. 

7. The concealer attack presentation non-match rate (CAPNMR) is the proportion of concealer attack presentations using the same PAI species in which the reference of the concealer is not matched. A concealer attack is an attack with the aim to evade from a recognition process e.g., by changing the facial expression or masquerade.

Several sensors can be implemented to realise a Presentation Attack Detection (PAD) without necessarily contributing to the image creation for biometric comparison. ## 2.3 Attack Vectors on a Biometric System 

In general, attack vectors are mechanisms and methods to overcome security features and compromise a system. The consequence is a partial or complete control of the system / device by an attacker. That means, an attacker can fully use all or some of the services mentioned in section System Model 1.4, like a valid user. The device can contain many different services and interfaces that may give rise to unauthorised access. The attack vectors in a generic device with a biometric authentication subsystem are described below. 

Current biometric systems, e.g., smartphones and tablets, mainly use two types of biometric modalities for verification, namely face and finger. Therefore, after a general description, the attack vectors targeting the two biometric verification modalities face and finger for authentication processes are described. 

A potential attacker can use several attack vectors to target the biometric verification subsystem: 

- Presentation of artificial / augmented biometric characteristics at the capture device level 

- Physical Adversarial Attacks against Face Recognition Systems [52] 

- Recording and replay or manipulation of transmitted information within or between the subsystems 

- Direct manipulation of the underlying algorithms (e.g., for template creation, feature extraction or similarity score calculation) 

- Insertion, deletion, modification of biometric templates at the biometric enrolment database 

- Threshold manipulation regarding similarity scores and PAD functionality

## 2.3.1.1 Presentation Attack 

According to ISO 30107-3 [7], a presentation attack is the attempt to present a biometric characteristic or object to the biometric data capture subsystem with the goal of interfering with the operation of the biometric system. 

## 2.3.1.2 Presentation Attack Instrument (PAI) 

For a presentation attack an attacker presents a presentation attack instrument (PAI) with the biometric characteristics of a genuine / authorised data subject to the capture device. 

To overcome the PAD mechanism, special presentation attack instruments (PAI), e.g., based on different materials can be used, which would not be detected as an artificial material and therefore not as an PAI. 

ATT-PAI-01 : A presentation attack instrument (PAI) SHOULD be created using a common production method fitting the biometric modality to attack. 

Note: In particular, for BAL 2 and 3, where an evaluator is free in creating new attack instruments within the defined attack potential, uncommon production methods can play an important role to evaluate the biometric system in a proper manner. 

ATT-PAI-02 : The creation procedure SHALL be based on the assumption of a worst-case scenario. 

Note: This means an attacker has access to the best and modality dependent data of the biometric subject to create the PAI. 

Typically, the first step is the acquisition of the biometric characteristic. Latent analogue samples stemming from the target biometric characteristics can be found for example on the device to attack itself or can be publicly available on the internet or be created by recording videos, images or other information sources. The quality of the biometric representation obtained can be improved via post-processing steps. A digitized version of the biometric characteristic can be translated into different PAIs made of different materials. This easiest kind of attack includes 2D projection / images of the biometric sample. To achieve PAIs of a higher quality, high-quality images (2D) and 3D data sets are necessary. Using these data sets, it is also possible to create artificial 3D PAIs (masks, 3D finger artefacts). Depending on the materials used and the sensor technology embedded in the capture device, the artificial PAI will create an image / signal at the biometric data capture subsystem or it will fail to do so. Therefore, it is necessary to implement PAD to detect artificial PAIs, which create a signal at the capture device. 

Further information on the production of presentation attack instruments (PAI) can be found in the Evaluation Guidance for this Technical Guideline. 

## 2.3.2 Information Transmission Attack 

Information has to be transmitted (see Figure 1 arrow 2, 4, 6, 8) both between different subsystems in the biometric system and between processes within individual subsystems. A potential attacker can manipulate the information at each transmission in order to influence the final decision. As an alternative, the attacker can store the information from a genuine authentication process and can replay this information to bypass the input of the biometric sample presented at the capture device. 

Based on the signal recorded during a genuine authentication process, this kind of replay attack can be performed between every pair of subsystems in order to achieve successful authentication.

The signal processing subsystem has multiple functions (PAD, quality control, feature extraction and segmentation). During an enrolment step, the biometric reference is created within the signal processor. 

Typically, PAD is one of the first steps in the signal processing subsystem. At this point, the attacker can change the PAD decision by tampering with the PAD subsystem. The signal of the PAI presented to the capture device is recognized for further processing.

Quality control might be deactivated by an attacker, which facilitates which would not have passed quality control.

This can also influence the process of reference creation, which is executed inside the signal processing subsystem. The reference might be based on biometric features unrelated to the presented biometric sample. 

## 2.3.4 Biometric Enrolment Database Attack 

After successful enrolment, the created biometric reference is stored in the biometric enrolment database. Typically, the database does not store the captured biometric reference itself, but rather a template containing the biometric features that are recognized and extracted. These steps are performed in the signal processor subsystem. 

At the biometric enrolment database level, different attacks can be executed. On the one hand, the attacker can add or modify biometric templates if the data format in which the biometric references are stored is known. On the other hand, the attacker can delete stored biometric references. 

## 2.3.5 Comparison Subsystem Attack 

The comparator in the comparison subsystem compares the stored biometric reference of the claimed identity with the biometric probe from the claimant. The comparator computes a numeric comparison score based on a specific metric. The comparator can be manipulated by an attacker to generate a false comparison score. 

## 2.3.5.1 Zero Effort Impostor Attack 

In case of the zero effort impostor attack an attacker presents his biometric characteristics. The biometric probe will be compared against the biometric reference enrolled by an authorised data subject. 

If the sensitivity / specificity of the capture device or the biometric comparison algorithm is not high enough or biometric samples contain intrinsically comparable biometric features, presented biometric characteristics can impersonate another individual. 

No PAD will be successful against this type of attack because, e.g., a real finger from a live person is presented to the capture device. 

Therefore, the matching algorithm has to operate with a sufficient performance, e.g., by finding a sufficient numbers of matching biometric features to obtain a biometric performance that matches the requirements of the desired BAL.

## 2.3.6 Comparison Decision Subsystem Attack 

The decision subsystem receives the comparison score from the comparison subsystem and decides, based on a pre-defined threshold, if the score is sufficiently high to accept it. This decision is binary. The final decision on the verification outcome for authentication is based on the verification policy. The verification decision for authentication is passed to the calling service. 

An attacker can proceed in two different ways to influence the final verification decision. Either the threshold can be manipulated in order to obtain a positive decision on verification, or the final decision after the comparison between score and threshold is manipulated.

## 4 Capture of Biometric Characteristics and Enrolment Processes (Informative) 

## 4.1 Modality Finger 

The biometric authentication subsystem can provide a sufficiently large sensor area in the capture device, which allows additional presentation methods. 

Using a sufficiently large fingerprint sensor area, it is possible to capture a rolled fingerprint, which could contain a higher number of biometric features (i.e., minutiae), which could contribute to achieve a lower FAR. In addition to the lower FAR, the creation of an artefact is more challenging due to the necessary 3D information. 

In case of a sufficiently large sensor capture device area, two or more fingerprints can be acquired at the same time. 

## 4.2 Enrolment Quality Recommendation 

To use biometrics with different modalities and instances as an authentication factor, the initial step is the enrolment. The quality of the captured biometric sample is the fundamental basis for the overall success of a biometric verification. Therefore, the enrolment can be performed in a manner obtaining biometric samples providing as many biometric features as possible. An upper bound for the entropy contained in the enrolled biometric template for fingerprints can be calculated for the more realistic model as shown by Ratha et al. [54]. The theoretically achievable entropy of a biometric sample acquired in the enrolment process is not a measure for a security evaluation. 

In practice, the number of recognized biometric features in a biometric capture represents only a subset of the biometric features in the most cases. Therefore, the entropy of every biometric capture will not exceed the entropy of the biometric reference. The captured area and the image quality effect the total number of recognizable biometric features. A lower bound for the entropy can be estimated on basis of the specific biometric modality used and the use case. 

The given approximation by Ratha et al. [54] can only be applied to the biometric modality finger. A comparable estimation of the information of a frontal face for the modality face is still under debate. Adler et al. [55] estimate the relative entropy, better known as Kullback-Leibler divergence, of a facial image of ~ 55 bit. A similar relative entropy-based estimation by Jassim et al. [56] taking different feature extraction techniques and image qualities into account. Depending on the applied feature extraction and the image quality, the relative entropy according to this publication is in the range of ~ 15 to 334 bits. 

Due to different constrains and assumptions it is hard to generalize such findings for a requirement regarding the information content of a facial image. In the future new generalized approaches may be discovered, which can be applied and a requirement can be derived for the quality of biometric data. As stated before the relative entropy of the biometric characteristic is not a measure for the security of the device. 

For devices with a long lifecycle, a regular re-enrolment can be an option to preserve the biometric performance.

## 4.3 Re-enrolment and Suspension of a Biometric Characteristic 

## Re-enrolment 

A re-enrolment of a biometric characteristic can be useful to preserve the biometric performance of the biometric verification system for authentication. Initialization of a re-enrolment can be performed by presenting a former enrolled and still valid biometric characteristic or a valid knowledge- or possessionbased authentication factor with an equivalent or better assurance level, e.g., eIDAS or BAL. The enrolled data subject can have the possibility to enrol a new biometric characteristic or the same instance. 

Example: The enrolled reference of the data subject can present a sufficient number of biometric characteristics, but due to temporal or permanent changes in the biometric characteristic the performance is not sufficient to verify the user with an existing reference. In addition, it can be possible that the data subject enrols a biometric characteristic with a sufficient number of features, but in everyday life the data subject uses different parts of the characteristic for authentication by verification. In both cases a reenrolment of the used characteristic can preserve the biometric performance and the usability of the biometric authentication subsystem. 

## Suspension 

Suspension is a temporal action, which prohibit the use of the biometric reference and the corresponding biometric instance. The stored biometric reference will not be deleted and can be re-activated by the user by presenting a different biometric characteristic, possession- or knowledge-based authentication factor from the same assurance level or higher. 

Example: The owner of the device can share this device with other user and restricts temporarily their authentication to the device and re-activates the authentication at a later date.

## A2. Improving Biometrics Usability 

This subsection has an informative character and gives advice to hard- and software developers on which general factors can improve the usability of biometrics as an authentication factor. The usability of a biometric authentication system can be linked to the overall system security or at least to the system performance. A frustrated user can circumvent integrated security measures, which can break new attack vectors to the authentication device. 

Ideally, if there is more than one biometric modality available that fulfils the requirements of the BAL required, the data subject can choose which modality suits them best. Different biometric modalities can differ in public acceptance and perceived convenience. 

The usability of the biometric modality might be increased by practicing the enrolment and authentication process with the biometric modality. 

Device affordances (i.e., properties of a device that allow a data subject to perform an action), feedback, and clear instructions are critical to a capture subjects success with the biometric process. For example, clear instructions on the actions required for a successful enrolment and use of the biometric modality are to provide. The instructions can be supported by clear and meaningful pictograms, or, if possible, by explanations in video clips. 

To avoid frustration, the system gives clear and meaningful feedback to the capture subject. During the authentication process, this could be done by displaying the remaining number of attempts before authentication will fall back to primary different authentication factor, or the remaining waiting time before the next attempt can be made. 

In addition, the system can inform the user if the biometric system has changed its behaviour due to switching to a different BAL (e.g., higher rejection rate due to increased biometric thresholds). This function might be implemented if different services requesting different BALs are accessed using the same biometric system. 

Example: The unlocking process of a mobile device implemented via a biometric authentication process requires BAL 1. After a successful first authentication, additional applications become available. Due to their higher risk potential, services like eGovernment or banking applications require BAL 2. During the starting process of the applications, a message informs the user that the change of the BAL required might cause a higher rejection rate due to the adjustment of settings to the BAL 2.

## A3. General Concepts for Improving Biometric Security 

This subsection has an informative character and gives advice to hard- and software developers on which general factors can improve usability and security of biometrics as an authentication factor. 

Devices with biometric authentication systems can be divided into different categories due to their embedded biometric capture devices. Depending on the intended use cases such systems can have capture subsystems for different biometric modalities like face and finger or support a single biometric modality via multiple technologies. For more information see 2.1.1.2 Multibiometrics. 

Ideally, if there is more than one biometric modality available that fulfils the requirements of the BAL required, the data subject can choose which modality suits them best. Different biometric modalities can differ in public acceptance and perceived convenience.

Measures as recommended in this section can be implemented by the developer and are easily accessible and adjustable to the user.
